The basics

The Pacific's warm–cool seesaw

Every two to seven years the across the tropical Pacific weaken or strengthen, shifting warm water east or west. That reshapes storms and ocean conditions thousands of miles away, including here.5

Neutral

Winds push warm water west; cooler water sits near the surface in the east.

El Niño

Winds weaken. Warm water and rainstorms spread east toward the Americas.

La Niña

Winds strengthen. Warm water piles up west; the east runs cold.

San Diego & Torrey Pines

What it means for our coast

Warm water takes up more room, so El Niño raises the sea along our shore. Higher water lets waves and tides reach farther up beaches and bluffs.14,15

Higher water

Past strong El Niños lifted our sea level 6–12 inches. This fall, San Diego tides have run 12–18 inches above predictions.22,23,24

Bigger waves

El Niño steers more winter storms toward California. In 2015–16, winter wave energy matched or beat the highest ever measured on the West Coast.9

Beaches and bluffs erode

In 2015–16, Torrey Pines State Beach dropped below its lowest level in seven years of surveys. Waves undercut local bluffs while rain wears away the top.9,10,11

Rain: odds, not promises

Big El Niños tilt Southern California toward wet late winters, but 2015–16 left San Diego drier than normal.8,18

The bigger picture

Climate change

Glossary

ENSO

ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation) is the tropical Pacific's natural cycle between a warm phase (El Niño), a cool phase (La Niña) and neutral, every two to seven years. It is the biggest year-to-year driver of climate on Earth.

Source [5]

Glossary

El Niño

El Niño is the warm phase of ENSO. Trade winds weaken, warm surface water spreads east across the tropical Pacific, and storm activity shifts with it. It is declared when the central Pacific stays at least 0.5°C warmer than normal.

Source [4]

Glossary

La Niña

La Niña is the cool phase of ENSO. Trade winds strengthen, warm water piles up in the west, and cold water rises in the eastern Pacific. Late 2025 and early 2026 were La Niña, just before this El Niño.

Source [3]

Glossary

NOAA's El Niño index (RONI)

RONI has been NOAA's official yardstick for El Niño since February 2026. It measures how much warmer or cooler than normal the 'Niño 3.4' box of the central Pacific is, relative to the rest of the tropics, so overall global warming doesn't skew it. +0.5°C means El Niño; +2.0°C or more is very strong.

Glossary

Sea surface height

Sea surface height is the level of the ocean surface, as measured by satellites and tide gauges. Warm water expands, so El Niño raises the sea surface across the eastern Pacific and along the California coast, letting tides and waves reach farther up the beach.

Source [15] · Learn more ↗

Glossary

Trade winds

Trade winds are steady winds that blow east to west across the tropical Pacific, piling warm water up near Asia. When they weaken, warm water drifts back east and El Niño can grow, which weakens the winds further.

Source [5]

Glossary

Kelvin wave

A Kelvin wave is not a surfing wave. It is a huge, slow 'slosh' of warm water that travels east along the equator below the surface, taking 2–3 months to cross the Pacific. Forecasters watch them as an early sign of El Niño.

Source [13] · Learn more ↗

Glossary

Teleconnection

A teleconnection is a long-distance link through the atmosphere. El Niño's tropical storms set off a chain of high- and low-pressure areas that carries its influence thousands of miles, to Southern California and around the world.

Source [5]

Glossary

Atmospheric river

An atmospheric river is a long, narrow band of water vapor that flows through the lower atmosphere, often from the tropics, like a river in the sky. When one meets the coast and mountains it can drop heavy rain. A few of these storms bring much of California's rain in a typical year.

Source [19]

Glossary

Marine heatwave

A marine heatwave is a stretch of days to years when ocean temperatures in a region stay far above normal. The U.S. West Coast has been in one since late 2025, and this El Niño is stacking on top of it, stressing kelp, fish and seabirds.

Source [14]

Glossary

King tide

King tides are the highest tides of the year, usually in winter. With today's higher seas, plus El Niño's extra warm water, they can flood low-lying streets and beaches on calm, sunny days.

Source [12] · Learn more ↗

Glossary

CoSMoS

CoSMoS is the USGS Coastal Storm Modeling System. Its computer maps show where flooding, beach erosion and cliff retreat could reach under different amounts of sea-level rise and storms. California State Parks and local agencies use it for planning, and anyone can view it free on Our Coast, Our Future.

Source [16] · Learn more ↗

Glossary

Southern Oscillation

The Southern Oscillation is the atmosphere's half of ENSO: a seesaw in atmospheric pressure between the western Pacific, measured at Darwin, Australia, and the central Pacific, measured at Tahiti. During El Niño, pressure runs higher than normal at Darwin and lower at Tahiti, the trade winds weaken, and the Southern Oscillation Index turns negative. La Niña flips the pattern.
